> **Playbook 2 · v1 — drafted from Contentathon #3 (1 July 2026).** Grounded in real objections from students, PhDs and the RDM professionals who support them. To be tested and refined at the October rollouts.

**Who you're advocating to.** Someone doing the actual work, generating the data, never formally taught FAIR — and it isn't graded. Their honest inner voice: *"What? Another thing I need to do? My supervisor knows nothing about this, so where do I start?"*

## What actually stops them

The barriers they voted most important:

| Their words | Why it bites |
|---|---|
| *"I've no time alongside my thesis."* | Real or perceived, it's the top worry. |
| *"There are no instructions — and is this even my responsibility as a PhD?"* | No lab support, sometimes supervisor opposition. |
| *"It's dry admin, not 'real' research."* | FAIR competes with the science they came to do. |
| *"My supervisor doesn't do it — why would I?"* | Going against the current is hard. |
| *"My data's public — what if there's something wrong?"* | Fear of exposure, not just apathy. |

## What lands (the hooks that answer them)

The framings the room judged strongest:

- **Save your future self.** Organised, catalogued data makes *writing the thesis* far easier and the data easier to explain to a supervisor. Sell time saved, not virtue.
- **Piggyback what they already do.** Most students already back up religiously — frame FAIR as *"the backup you already do, plus a little cataloguing and metadata."* No new behaviour, just better.
- **"FAIR enough for your thesis."** Push back on scary, complicated "proper FAIR" — give a short checklist of what their uni/community actually needs, and stop there.
- **Make it about them, not others.** "Helps future reusers" is not an incentive; "helps *you*, now" is.
- The reframes that opened it up: *How might we make a time-poor student see FAIR as saving them stress? · Give them something practical without having to understand all of FAIR · Make FAIR a normal part of research, not a bolt-on.*

## How to deliver it

**The organise-vs-chaos race.** One group keeps its data organised as it goes; the other doesn't. Then both have to "write it up" and share it — *which team is faster and less stressed?* Hands-on, and it makes the "saves you time" pitch self-evident. (Simplify any game to sell FAIR as a stress-*reducer*, not another demand.)

## What they actually say

> *"There's too much information on FAIR so I have no idea where to get started."*
> *"We'll worry about FAIR at the end — first, let's play with our data."*
> *"It's not exciting — it'll never be exciting."*

Use these verbatim. They tell you the pitch must lead with *ease* and *relevance*, never principles.
